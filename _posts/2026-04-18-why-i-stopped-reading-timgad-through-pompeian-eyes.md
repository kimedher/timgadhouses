For a long time, anyone who wanted to talk about a Roman house started with Pompeii. Atrium, peristyle, axial sightline, cubicula arranged around the open court. It is a beautiful grammar, and it has also been the single most effective way to misread the houses of Roman North Africa.

I know this because for a while I was doing it too.

When I first started reading on Timgad, I looked for the familiar shapes. Where was the atrium? Was the peristyle central or offset? Did the main axis of sight run from the threshold straight through to a garden? I was, without meaning to, treating the Italian elite house as a template and asking where Timgad fell short. A lot of published scholarship does this, and quietly. You can feel it in the captions: *a modest example*, *irregular in plan*, *lacking the expected sequence*. Modest, irregular, lacking. The vocabulary is doing work.

The work it is doing is called a legacy, and it goes back to Vitruvius. His *De Architectura*, written in the first century BCE, describes a very particular kind of house: one suited to metropolitan, male, elite Roman life, organized around the atrium-peristyle sequence, with rooms arranged to manage patron-client rituals. Vitruvius is describing an ideal, not a survey. He is telling his reader what a house *should* look like if it is meant to signal elite belonging at the center of the empire. Two thousand years later, that ideal has become our measuring stick, and every provincial house that does not match it becomes, by definition, deficient.

## What you see if you stop measuring

The moment I stopped reading Timgad through Pompeian eyes, the houses got more interesting, not less.

What actually sits inside Timgad's insulae is a domestic landscape where production and home life share walls. Twelve fullonicae, the workshops where cloth was cleaned and finished, were built directly into residential blocks. Olive presses sit in courtyards. Textile dyeing stations occupy rooms that in a Pompeian house would be bedrooms or dining rooms. In some cases, a single architectural unit contains a workshop, a family's sleeping quarters, and a small peristyle, all threaded together by the same corridor.

You can read this as chaos if you are determined to read it that way. Or you can read it as a different kind of order, one where the line between domestic and economic life was not supposed to be sharp, because the rhythm of daily life did not require that line to be sharp. People in Timgad moved between production and family activity several times a day. The walls of their houses made that movement possible. The spaces they built materialize a different set of assumptions about what a home is.

## The trouble with "failed imitation"

Treating these houses as failed imitations has two problems.

The first is empirical. It forces us to ignore most of what is actually there. If you only count a room as significant when it matches a Vitruvian category, an enormous amount of Timgad's domestic archaeology becomes invisible. The dyeing vats, the *calcatoria*, the workshops pressed against the triclinia: none of these read as anything under the old template, because the template has no slot for them.

The second is interpretive. If you assume the local inhabitants were trying and failing to build Pompeian houses, you have already decided what they were trying to do. You have taken away their agency before you started looking. And it turns out, when you look carefully, that they were not trying to build Pompeii. They were solving a different set of problems, in a different climate, with a different labor economy, for a population that included legionary veterans, local Numidians, workshop owners, and workers whose identities overlapped and intersected in daily practice.

This is where the language of habitus becomes useful. Bourdieu developed the concept partly through studying Kabylie houses in Algeria, which is to say, partly through studying houses a few hundred kilometers from Timgad, in a landscape not unlike the Aurès. Houses, in his reading, are not backdrops to social life. They are active agents that produce the dispositions, the embodied habits, of the people who move through them. If Timgad's houses made room for production to live next to family, that choice was shaping something about how the people who lived in them understood work, home, and themselves.

## The second problem: colonial eyes

There is a further layer. Most of what we know about Timgad's houses comes from French colonial excavations between the 1880s and 1950s. These excavators, starting with Albert Ballu, saw themselves as inheritors of Rome's "civilizing mission." Their reports are thorough, indispensable, and also partial in ways that matter. They privileged the buildings and features that fit their sense of what a Roman city should look like. They reconstructed walls and thresholds and mosaics along the lines of what they expected. They paid less attention, and devoted less documentation, to the features that complicated the picture, including the workshops in the domus.

This is not a reason to throw out the excavation reports. They are still most of the evidence base, and they were made by people who worked hard under difficult conditions. It is a reason to read them carefully, with awareness of the frame they were working in, and to supplement them wherever possible with unpublished archival material and with what we can still see on the site today.

## What I am doing instead

What I am doing now, and what this site exists to support, is a different starting move. Instead of asking how closely a Timgad house resembles a Pompeian one, I ask what the Timgad house made possible for the people who lived in it. Instead of treating workshops-in-houses as exceptions to be explained away, I treat them as evidence of an integrated domestic economy, and I ask what that integration did to daily life, to encounters between family and worker, to the rhythms of labor, sound, and smell.

This is slower work than the old framework allowed, because it requires treating every house on its own terms rather than sorting it into a preexisting type. It is also, I think, more honest. Timgad's houses are not defective Pompeiis. They are provincial Roman homes, shaped by their time and place, built and inhabited by people who were making something particular. They deserve to be read that way.
