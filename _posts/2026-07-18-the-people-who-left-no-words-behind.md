Most of history was written by a small and powerful few.

Emperors, generals, senators, the handful of men wealthy enough to be remembered on purpose. They commissioned the monuments, wrote the histories, and carved their names into stone that was meant, quite deliberately, to outlast them. And to give credit where it is due, it worked, because here we are two thousand years later still saying their names. But they were never most people. They were not even close to most people.

Everyone else left almost nothing in writing. The workers, the women, the enslaved, the shopkeepers, the provincials living at the far edges of an empire that was built on their backs, that ran on their work, and that they never chose. History as it was written down mostly passes them over, and not because their lives were empty, their lives were full of work and family and worry and small daily pleasures the same way ours are, but because almost nobody holding a pen thought those lives were worth recording. If we only had texts, we would know an empire entirely through the people who ran it, and almost not at all through the people who lived in it.

This is where archaeology comes in, and it is the whole reason I do what I do. I get asked some version of "so, why archaeology" fairly often, at family dinners, at events when someone finds out what my degree is actually in (after asking if I'm like Indiana Jones of course), from students who are trying to figure out what to do with their own lives, and I usually give the short, polite, elevator version. This post is the longer and more honest one.

## Why archaeology

Archaeology is how we read the lives of people who left no words behind. A text can flatter, exaggerate, or quietly erase whoever the writer wanted forgotten, and writers have been doing exactly that for as long as there has been writing. A worn threshold cannot, and by threshold I mean the literal stone sill of a doorway, the block you step over to pass from one room into the next, worn down in the middle because thousands of real feet crossed it, day after day, year after year, until the stone itself became a record of which door mattered. A cooking hearth, a repaired wall, a workshop wedged into the corner of a family home, a mosaic laid by hands we will never be able to name, these things are testimony too, and unlike the texts, they include everyone. The powerful and the ordinary left the same kind of evidence in the ground, the same broken pottery, the same doorways, the same floors worn smooth in the exact places where feet actually landed, which makes the material record far more democratic than the written one ever was.

That is what hooked me, and it still gets me, years in. Archaeology is the closest thing we have to hearing directly from the people history left out. Not their words, those are gone and no amount of wishing brings them back, but their choices, their routines, their repairs and renovations and workarounds, the marks their daily lives pressed into the spaces around them. You learn to read those marks a little like you would read a letter, slowly, carefully, and with the constant awareness that you might be getting it wrong.

## Why houses

If archaeology is how we reach ordinary people, houses are where we actually find them.

  <figcaption>Plan of Insula 30 at Timgad. The <em>fullonica</em>, a cloth-working shop, sits in the north-east corner, sharing its walls with the rooms of a home. Plan: T. Amraoui.</figcaption>
</figure>

A monument tells you what an empire wanted remembered. A house tells you how people actually lived, and those are almost never the same story. The Roman word for it, *domus*, did not even mean the building, it meant the household, everyone who lived and worked inside it, from the head of the family down to the workers and the enslaved people the home depended on. The walls and the people were a single idea, which is something I think about constantly, because it means that when I trace the outline of a house I am not really drawing architecture, I am drawing the outline of a household.

Take the plan above, a single *insula*, one city block, at Timgad. Tucked into its north-east corner is a *fullonica*, a workshop where cloth was washed and finished, sitting right in among the rooms people lived in, sharing their walls, part of the same block of daily life. Think of the family that runs the convenience store downstairs and lives in the apartment above it, the shop and the home sharing a staircase and a set of walls. The arrangement here is much the same, just two thousand years older. Work and home were not separate worlds here, a family and its trade often lived under one roof, and you do not need a single written word to see it. It is right there in the plan.

That is why a floor plan is so much more than a floor plan. The way rooms open onto one another, which spaces were dressed up to be seen and which were kept back for the family, where the cooking happened, where the workshop sat, where a guest was received and where they most definitely were not, all of it is a record of relationships, work, status, and daily rhythm. You can read a whole society in the arrangement of a house, if you know how to look, and I will be the first to admit that knowing how to look is a skill I am still very much practicing.

## Why Timgad, and why cultural interaction

I read these questions at Timgad, a Roman city founded around 100 CE on a high plain at the edge of the Sahara, in what is now Algeria. If you have spent any time on this site you have already met it, this whole project is built around its houses, but it is worth saying how I ended up there, because the road was not direct.

I got my start digging in Roman Spain, another province, another far edge of the same empire, and I have been drawn to the provinces ever since. Then a course on Roman North Africa introduced me to a part of the Roman world my training had mostly passed over, and that was that. The course led me to Timgad, and I have not really left since.

What draws me there is that Timgad was never simply Roman. It was a provincial city where an imperial template met local North African life and the two had to work something out, street by street, house by house. Veterans, workshop owners, and local Numidians shared its streets and reshaped its houses to fit lives the original plan never anticipated. Homes turned inward around shaded courtyards against the heat. Work and livelihood were folded into domestic space, so a family and its trade often shared the same walls, exactly like that fullery in Insula 30. Reading these houses through the lens of Pompeii, as if one Italian town could explain a thousand provincial lives, misses almost everything that made them theirs, and pushing back on that habit is, in one sentence, what my dissertation is about.

That negotiation, between the order an empire imposes and the way people actually live inside it, is the heart of my work. Houses are where you can watch it happen, one room at a time.

## The bigger why

Underneath all of this sits a question that is as alive now as it was two thousand years ago: how people make a home inside an empire they did not choose, and how the spaces we build end up shaping who we become.

I have stood in the Colosseum like every other person who loves this field, and I would not trade that day for anything, but I remember looking out over the arena and thinking that this is not where the story of most Romans actually happened. It happened in kitchens and courtyards and workshops, in rooms no poet ever bothered to mention, in houses like the ones I now spend my days tracing wall by wall. We tend to think we shape our spaces, and we do, but they shape us right back. The rooms we move through, the thresholds we cross, the walls we share with neighbors we did not pick, they quietly teach us who we are and where we belong. That was true for a Numidian family in Roman Timgad, and it is true for you, right now, in whatever room you are reading this in.

So when people ask why archaeology, or why houses, this is the honest answer. It is how I get to sit with the people who were never written down, and let the places they lived speak for them. The words are gone. The homes are still talking.
