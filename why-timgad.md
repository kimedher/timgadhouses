Timgad is a Roman city in modern-day Algeria, set on a high plain at the foot of the Aurès mountains, about 500 kilometers southeast of the capital, Algiers. The Romans called it Thamugadi. It was founded in 100 CE by the emperor Trajan as a colony for veterans of the Third Augustan legion, and over the next three centuries it grew into a substantial provincial city before being abandoned in the Middle Ages. Buried by sand for centuries, it is one of the best-preserved Roman sites anywhere, and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1982.

## A city laid out on a grid

What makes Timgad unusual is how legible it is. The city was planned on a strict grid, with two main streets crossing at right angles and everything else falling into regular rectangular blocks. Unlike most Roman cities, which grew over centuries and then kept growing, Timgad was laid down in one go and never substantially replanned during its early centuries. Walking through it today, you can still read the original street plan under your feet.

That clarity is why this project is possible. With the city grid intact, we can place each excavated house on a map and start asking what patterns appear in how houses were arranged, which blocks were dense with workshops, which streets concentrated wealth, and where the evidence is thinnest. The interactive grid on this site is the first step in that work.

## Around one hundred houses, and why that matters

Timgad preserves roughly one hundred excavated houses, one of the largest assemblages of domestic architecture in Roman North Africa. Despite this, only about five of them have been analyzed in any meaningful detail in scholarship so far. That gap is most of the reason this project exists.

The houses at Timgad are not the elegant peristyle villas you might recognize from museum galleries or textbook images of Pompeii. Many of them blur the line between home and workplace in ways that do not fit the tidy categories Roman architecture textbooks prefer. Fullonicae, which are workshops where cloth was cleaned and finished, were built directly into houses. Olive presses appeared in courtyards. The smells, sounds, and rhythms of production moved through rooms where families ate and slept.

That integration is not a quirk. It is evidence of how provincial life actually worked during a period that economic historians call the African boom, the second to fifth centuries when North Africa became the empire's breadbasket and a major center of oil, grain, and textile production. Timgad sits at the heart of that transformation.

## Why this matters now

The question of how homes shape the people who live in them is not only a question about antiquity. Anyone who worked from home during the pandemic knows what it feels like when domestic life, economic production, and social identity all happen in the same rooms. Timgad's inhabitants lived something similar, eighteen centuries ago, and the shape of their houses both responded to those pressures and shaped how they answered them. Paying attention to these houses is a way of paying attention to how people, then and now, negotiate belonging, labor, and home under the constraints they are given.

## Reading the evidence carefully

One reason Timgad has been under-read is that most of its houses were excavated by French colonial archaeologists between the 1880s and the 1950s. These excavators saw themselves as inheritors of Rome's "civilizing mission," and it shaped what they recorded, what they ignored, and what they reconstructed. As recent scholarship has shown, colonial excavation reports are not neutral documents. They are artifacts of the moment they were made.

Working with Timgad responsibly means reading those reports critically, filling in what they left out, and where possible checking them against what we can see on the ground and in unpublished archives. It also means giving the city its pre-Roman and modern names the attention they deserve. Timgad is Thamugadi in Latin, but it is also a place in Algeria today, with its own post-ancient history, and this project tries to hold those pasts together rather than collapsing them into one.
